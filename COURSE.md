## Slide 5: Somali Vowels — The 5 Basic Sounds

Somali has 5 vowels:

| Vowel | English Sound | Somali Example |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| **A** | as in "FATHER" | **aabo** (father) |
| **E** | as in "BED" | **erey** (word) |
| **I** | as in "KEEP" | **ilma** (to know) |
| **O** | as in "GO" | **omar** (age) |
| **U** | as in "BOOK" | **ubax** (flower) |

**THE CRITICAL DIFFERENCE:**

- **Short vowel:** a → dal (country)
- **Long vowel:** aa → daal (lentils)

Same vowel. Different length. Different word.

## Slide 8: Somali Consonants — The Regular Ones

From our dialogue, we heard many consonants. Let's identify them:

**Consonants are sounds WITH obstacles to airflow:**

| Type | Examples | From Dialogue |
|------|----------|---------------|
| Stops (air blocked) | B, D, G, K, P, T | **D**al, **G**abar, **T**aada |
| Fricatives (air hisses) | F, H, S, Z, J, **SH** | **H**adii, **J**oggaa, **SH**aqaysaa |
| Nasals (air through nose) | M, N | **M**agacaagu, **N**inka |
| Approximants (smooth) | L, R, W, Y | **W**alaal, **R**un (know) |

Some consonants are familiar. Some are unique to Somali. Next slides: we focus on those.

## Slide 11: Deep Throat Sounds — Somali Originals

Somali has sounds that don't exist in English. They come from deep in the throat:

**Q** — Deep K (very back of throat)
- Example: qalin (pen), qof (person)
- Sound: Like K, but MUCH deeper

**X** — Harsh H (deep and forceful)
- Example: xaal (condition), xalay (yesterday)
- Sound: Like H, but from the throat

**DH** — Soft D + H blend (single sound)
- Example: dhacay (fell), dhaaf (passed)
- Sound: NOT "D then H" — one blended sound

**KH** — Guttural (like German "Bach")
- Example: khaat (chat plant), khan (inn)
- Sound: Like the "ch" in German or Spanish "jota"

These sounds DEFINE Somali's character.

## Slide 14: Stress & Rhythm — Where's the Emphasis?

In English, stress is unpredictable:
- **PRE**sent (noun) vs. pre**SENT** (verb)

In Somali, stress is more regular:
- Usually on the FIRST syllable
- Sometimes on the LAST syllable
- Pattern is predictable

**From our dialogue:**

- **MAG**-a-caa-gu (name) → 1st syllable
- **SKU**-ul-ka (school) → 1st syllable
- **SHA**-qa-ysaa (you work) → 1st syllable
- **JOO**-gaa (be present) → 1st syllable

Listen to Somali speakers. Notice where the emphasis lands.

## Slide 17: Syllable Structure — How Words Are Built

Words are built from syllables. Somali follows predictable patterns:

| Pattern | Meaning | Examples |
|---------|---------|----------|
| **V** | Vowel alone | a (he/she), o (yes) |
| **CV** | Consonant + Vowel | ba (no), mo (from), ga (said) |
| **CVC** | C + V + C | cat (dew), gal (enter), qof (person) |
| **CVVC** | C + V + V + C | qaaf (palm), saaq (leg) |
| **CVCC** | C + V + C + C | farsh (bed), nafs (soul) |

**Pattern NOT allowed:** CC alone — Somali doesn't start words with two consonants without a vowel between.

**CONSONANTS (key list):**

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Introducing Nouns

**Listen and notice which words are PEOPLE, THINGS, or IDEAS:**

```
FADUMA: Salaam, walaalkay! Sidee tahay?
        (Hello, my brother! How are you?)

AHMED: Waa iska warran. Naag iyo wiil baa jira.
       (I'm fine. There's a woman and a boy.)

FADUMA: Magaalo baa jirtaa?
        (Is there a city?)

AHMED: Hah, magaalada Mogadishu waa weyn.
       (Yes, the city of Mogadishu is big.)

FADUMA: Gabar baa la dhahay?
        (Do you mean a girl?)

AHMED: Hah, gabar yar baa jirtaa. Ninkaa baa caraana.
       (Yes, there's a small girl. The man is tired.)
```

**Notice:** Some words describe people (naag, wiil, gabar, nin). Others describe places (magaalada). Some describe qualities (weyn = big, yar = small).

## Slide 4: What Are Nouns?

**Nouns are words that name:**

- **PEOPLE:** nin (man), gabar (girl), haween (woman), wiil (boy)
- **THINGS:** magaalo (city), guri (house), baabuur (car), kitaab (book)
- **IDEAS:** habab (manner), xaal (condition), fikir (thought)
- **ABSTRACT:** nur (light), mugdi (darkness), xisaab (account)

In Somali, **EVERY noun has a GENDER.**

Either **masculine** or **feminine.**

This is FUNDAMENTAL to how Somali works.

## Slide 5: The Gender System — Introduction

**Somali nouns are either MASCULINE or FEMININE.**

This is not random — there are patterns and rules.

**Gender affects:**
- How you say "the" before a noun
- How adjectives agree with the noun
- How pronouns refer to the noun
- How verbs conjugate when the noun is the subject

**Gender is PERMANENT for each noun.**
- nin (man) is ALWAYS masculine
- gabar (girl) is ALWAYS feminine
- magaalo (city) is ALWAYS feminine

## Slide 8: Masculine Nouns — Recognition & Markers

**Masculine nouns often have these patterns:**

- **Ending in -∅ (no special ending):** nin (man), wiil (boy), gaac (camel), dog (sheep)
- **Ending in -e:** nibe (thief), sida/side (way/manner)
- **Ending in -i:** ladi (milk container), dimbi (guilt)

**Examples from dialogue:**
- **nin** (man) — MASCULINE
- **wiil** (boy) — MASCULINE
- **ahmed** (proper name) — MASCULINE

**How to recognize:** No special feminine marker (like -a or -ta at the end)

## Slide 9: Feminine Nouns — Recognition & Markers

**Feminine nouns often end in these patterns:**

- **Ending in -a (short):** gabar (girl), naag (woman), magaalo (city), katabo (book - in some contexts)
- **Ending in -ad:** shahaada (testimony), duulad (crowd)
- **Ending in -o:** caro (tiredness), shido (hardship)

**Examples from dialogue:**
- **gabar** (girl) — FEMININE (ends in -a)
- **naag** (woman) — FEMININE (ends in -a)
- **magaalo** (city) — FEMININE (ends in -a)
- **haween** (woman/wife) — FEMININE (ends in -een)

**Pattern:** Many feminine nouns end in **-a** or **-o**.

## Slide 12: Gender Markers in Context

**When a noun is DEFINITE (the + noun), gender shows in the marker:**

| Gender | Marker | Example | Meaning |
|--------|--------|---------|---------|
| MASCULINE | -ka | nin-ka | **the** man |
| MASCULINE | -ka | wiil-ka | **the** boy |
| FEMININE | -ta | gabar-ta | **the** girl |
| FEMININE | -ta | magaalo-ta | **the** city |

**Important:** The -ka and -ta are **not** part of the noun itself. They're ARTICLE markers.

- nin = man (indefinite)
- nin-ka = the man (definite)
- gabar = girl (indefinite)
- gabar-ta = the girl (definite)

## Slide 15: Pluralization — From Singular to Plural

**In Somali, plurals are formed by CHANGING the noun's ending.**

**Most common pluralization patterns:**

| Singular | Plural | Rule |
|----------|--------|------|
| nin | niman | -∅ → -an |
| wiil | wiilal | -∅ → -al |
| gabar | gabar-yaal | -ar → -ar-yaal |
| naag | naag-yaal | -ag → -ag-yaal |
| magaalo | magaa-lo | some plurals don't change much |
| kitaab | kitaab-yada | -ab → -ab-yada |

**Key:** Plurals are NOT just adding 's'. The ENTIRE ending changes.

## Slide 16: Masculine Plurals

**Masculine nouns commonly pluralize with:**

- **-an ending:** nin → niman (man → men), wiil → wilal (boy → boys)
- **-aal ending:** gaac → gaacaal (camel → camels)
- **-yada ending:** kitaab → kitaab-yada (book → books)

**Examples:**
- nin (man) → niman (men)
- wiil (boy) → wilal (boys)
- araweelo (old woman's name) → not typically pluralized (proper noun)

## Slide 17: Feminine Plurals

**Feminine nouns commonly pluralize with:**

- **-yaal ending:** gabar → gabar-yaal (girl → girls), naag → naag-yaal (woman → women)
- **-o ending:** magaalo → magaaloyin (cities)
- **-ada ending:** shahaada → shahaad-ada (testimonies)

**Examples:**
- gabar (girl) → gabar-yaal (girls)
- naag (woman) → naag-yaal (women)
- magaalo (city) → magaaloyin (cities)

## Slide 20: Gender & Number in Action

**When a noun is both GENDERED and NUMBERED (plural), the markers combine:**

| Singular | Plural (Indefinite) | Plural (Definite) |
|----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| nin-ka (the man) | niman (men) | niman-ka (the men) |

| wiil-ka (the boy) | wilal (boys) | wilal-ka (the boys) |
| magaalo-ta (the city) | magaaloyin (cities) | magaaloyin-ka (the cities) |

**Notice:** Even plural nouns get the definite marker (-ka or -ta).

## Slide 21: Adjective Agreement — Nouns & Adjectives Work Together

**Adjectives MUST AGREE with nouns in GENDER and NUMBER.**

**Example:**

| Noun | Adjective | Together | Meaning |
|------|-----------|----------|---------|

| gabar yar | — | gabar yar | small girl |
| niman waaweyn | — | niman waaweyn | big men |

When you add an adjective, BOTH the noun and adjective must show the same gender.

## Slide 4: Definite vs. Indefinite — The Core Concept

**Every Somali noun is either DEFINITE or INDEFINITE.**

**DEFINITE:** The speaker is talking about a SPECIFIC noun (the one both people know about)
- nin-ka (THE specific man)
- gabar-ta (THE specific girl)
- niman-ka (THE specific men)

**INDEFINITE:** The speaker is talking about ANY noun of that type (not a specific one)
- nin (a man, any man)
- gabar (a girl, any girl)
- niman (men, any men)

**How you show it:**
- Definite: Add -ka (masculine) or -ta (feminine) → nin-ka, gabar-ta
- Indefinite: No marker → nin, gabar

This is the ARTICLE SYSTEM in Somali.

## Slide 5: The Article System — Gender Determines the Marker

**Remember from Module 2: Gender shows in articles!**

| Gender | Definite Singular | Definite Plural | Indefinite |
|--------|-------------------|-----------------|-----------|
| MASCULINE | nin-**ka** | niman-**ka** | nin |
| FEMININE | gabar-**ta** | gabar-yaal-**ka** | gabar |

**Key pattern:**
- Masculine articles use **-ka**
- Feminine articles use **-ta**
- Even plurals get articles (niman-ka, gabar-yaal-ka)

The article marker tells you both the gender AND whether it's definite.

## Slide 8: Demonstratives — This, That, These, Those

**Demonstratives are words that POINT to specific nouns:**

**NEAR (this, these):**
- kan (this one — masculine)
- tan (this one — feminine)
- kuwan (these — plural)

**FAR (that, those):**
- kaas (that one — masculine)
- taas (that one — feminine)
- kuwaas (those — plural)

**Examples:**
- Nin-**ka** = the man (definite)
- Nin-**kan** = **this** man (pointing: near)
- Nin-**kaas** = **that** man (pointing: far)

Notice: Demonstratives REPLACE the definite article, not add to it.

## Slide 9: Demonstratives in Dialogue

**Listen to how demonstratives are used:**

```

        nin-kaas = that man over there (far)

       gabar-tan = this specific girl (near)

FADUMA: Gabar-taas ayaa dhex jirtaa. (That girl is inside.)
        gabar-taas = that girl (far)

AHMED: Niman-kuwan way socdaan. (These men are going.)
       niman-kuwan = these men (plural, near)

FADUMA: Niman-kuwaas way seexdeen. (Those men are sleeping.)
        niman-kuwaas = those men (plural, far)
```

**Notice:** Demonstratives show DISTANCE (near vs. far) and GENDER.

**Three ways to specify a noun:**

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Pronouns in Use

**Listen and notice the pronouns (bolded):**

```
HASSAN: **Ani** waxaan malaha. (**I** think...)
        ani = I (subject pronoun)

AMINA: **Isaga** waa roon. (He is nice.)
       isaga = he (subject pronoun with emphasis)

HASSAN: **Ina** eeg! (Look at **me**!)
        ina = me (object pronoun)

AMINA: **Inaagu** magaca waa maxay? (What is **your** name?)
       inaagu = your (possessive pronoun)

HASSAN: **Kayaga** ayaa timid. (**They** came.)
        kayaga = they (plural)

AMINA: **Isaga** inqaransho, wuu wacyi karti. (**He** himself, he can do it.)
```

**Question:** Do pronouns change based on gender like nouns do?

## Slide 4: What Are Pronouns?

**Pronouns are words that REPLACE nouns.**

Instead of saying:
- "Nin-ka baa timid. Nin-ka wuu ciyaara." (The man came. The man plays.)

You say:
- "Nin-ka baa timid. **Isaga** wuu ciyaara." (The man came. **He** plays.)

**Why use pronouns?**
- Avoid repeating the same noun over and over
- Make conversation smoother and more natural
- Reference people already mentioned

**Types of pronouns in Somali:**
- Personal pronouns (I, you, he, she, we, they)
- Possessive pronouns (my, your, his, her, our, their)
- Reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, etc.)
- Relative pronouns (who, which, that)

## Slide 5: Personal Pronouns — Subject Forms

**Subject pronouns are who is DOING the action:**

| Person | Singular | Plural |
|--------|----------|--------|
| 1st | **ani** (I) | **innaga** (we) |
| 2nd | **adiga** (you) | **idinka** (you all) |
| 3rd Masc | **isaga** (he) | **iyaga** (they) |
| 3rd Fem | **iyada** (she) | **iyaga** (they) |

**Key insight:** "They" (iyaga) is the SAME for both masculine and feminine plural!

**In sentences:**
- Ani waxaan shaqeynaa. (I work.)
- Adiga waad shaqeysaa. (You work.)
- Isaga wuu shaqeynaa. (He works.)

Notice the verb changes with the pronoun!

## Slide 6: Personal Pronouns — Object Forms

**Object pronouns are who is RECEIVING the action:**

| Person | Singular | Plural |
|--------|----------|--------|
| 1st | **ina** (me) | **ina** (us) |
| 2nd | **kaa** (you) | **idinka** (you all) |
| 3rd Masc | **isaga** (him) | **iyaga** (them) |
| 3rd Fem | **iyada** (her) | **iyaga** (them) |

**In sentences:**
- Waxaa ina qaatay. (Someone took **me**.)
- Waxaa kaa arki jiray. (They were seeing **you**.)
- Waxaa isaga gaystay. (Someone hit **him**.)

Notice: Object pronouns are usually placed AFTER the verb in Somali.

## Slide 9: Pronoun Agreement — Gender Matters

**Remember from Module 2: Some things agree with gender, others don't.**

**Pronouns for HE vs. SHE:**

When replacing a **masculine noun** (like nin = man):
- Use **isaga** (he) as subject
- Use **isaga** (him) as object

When replacing a **feminine noun** (like gabar = girl):
- Use **iyada** (she) as subject
- Use **iyada** (her) as object

**BUT:** When you don't know the gender or are talking about multiple people, use **iyaga** (they).

**Example:**
- Nin-ka wuu socdaa. → **Isaga** wuu socdaa. (The man goes. He goes.)
- Gabar-ta way socdataa. → **Iyada** way socdataa. (The girl goes. She goes.)

## Slide 12: Possessive Pronouns — My, Your, His, Her

**Possessive pronouns show OWNERSHIP or RELATIONSHIP:**

| Person | Singular | Plural |
|--------|----------|--------|
| 1st | **inaagu** (my) | **innaga** (our) |
| 2nd | **iyaagu** (your) | **idinka** (your) |
| 3rd Masc | **isaga** (his) | **iyaga** (their) |
| 3rd Fem | **iyada** (her) | **iyaga** (their) |

**Examples:**
- Kitaab-**inaagu** (my book)
- Guri-**iyaagu** (your house)
- Magaalo-**ta** (her city) — notice the feminine -ta article!
- Gabar-yaal-**iyaga** (their girls)

## Slide 13: Possessive Pronouns in Sentences

**Notice how possessive pronouns work with nouns:**

```

inaagu = my (possessive)
magaca = name
waa = is
Ahmed = Ahmed

Iyaagu guriga wuu weyn. (Your house is big.)
iyaagu = your (possessive)
guriga = the house
wuu = is (masculine)
weyn = big

Isaga ninka wuu malaya. (His man — his son/relative is weak.)
isaga = his (possessive)
```

**Pattern:** Possessive pronouns come BEFORE the noun they modify.

## Slide 16: Reflexive Pronouns — Myself, Yourself, Himself

**Reflexive pronouns show that the subject is ACTING ON THEMSELVES:**

| Person | Singular | Example |
|--------|----------|---------|
| 1st | **inaangoo** (myself) | Inaangoo qatay. (I took myself = I took it myself.) |
| 2nd | **adiga** (yourself) | Adiga qaata. (You take yourself = You do it yourself.) |
| 3rd Masc | **isaga** (himself) | Isaga qaatay. (He took himself = He did it himself.) |
| 3rd Fem | **iyada** (herself) | Iyada qaatay. (She took herself = She did it herself.) |

**Key difference from object pronouns:** Reflexives emphasize "DOING IT YOURSELF" not just "receiving the action."

5. **Derive** adjectives from other words

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Adjectives in Use

**Listen and notice how adjectives describe nouns:**

```
AMINA: Nin weyn baa jira. (A big man exists.)
       weyn = big (masculine form)

HASSAN: Gabar yar baa jirtaa. (A small girl exists.)
        yar = small (feminine form)

AMINA: Niman cusub baa timideen. (New men came.)
       cusub = new (plural form)

HASSAN: Gabar-yaal qaali baa socdaan. (Expensive girls are going.)
        qaali = expensive (feminine plural)

AMINA: Magaalo weyn iyo magaalo yar. (A big city and a small city.)
       Notice: weyn for big city, yar for small city (both feminine)
```

**Question:** Why does "big" change from weyn to weyn depending on the noun?

## Slide 4: What Are Adjectives?

**Adjectives are words that DESCRIBE nouns.**

They answer questions like:
- What SIZE? (big, small, tall, short)
- What COLOR? (black, white, red)
- What QUALITY? (good, bad, beautiful, ugly)
- What CONDITION? (new, old, fresh, rotten)

**Examples:**
- Nin weyn (big man)
- Gabar yar (small girl)
- Kitaab cusub (new book)
- Magaalo kalyar (small city)

**In Somali, adjectives come AFTER the noun they describe** (opposite of English):
- English: "big man"
- Somali: "man big" (nin weyn)

## Slide 5: Adjective Agreement — Gender & Number

**CRITICAL: Adjectives MUST AGREE with the noun's GENDER and NUMBER.**

This is the same rule as Articles (Module 3) and Pronouns (Module 4)!

**Example with "big" (weyn):**

| Noun | Adjective | Together | Gender |
|------|-----------|----------|--------|
| nin (man) | **weyn** | nin weyn | Masculine |
| gabar (girl) | **weyn** | gabar weyn | Feminine |
| niman (men) | **waaweyn** | niman waaweyn | Masculine Plural |
| gabar-yaal (girls) | **weyn** | gabar-yaal weyn | Feminine Plural |

**Pattern:** The SAME adjective can have different forms depending on the noun!

## Slide 6: Adjective Forms by Gender

**Somali adjectives have different forms for different genders:**

| Meaning | Masculine | Feminine | Plural |
|---------|-----------|----------|--------|
| big | weyn | weyn | waaweyn |
| small | yar | yar | yaraan |
| good | wanaagsan | wanaagsan | wanaagsan |
| new | cusub | cusub | cusub |
| old | gaduud | gaduud | gadduudan |

**Notice:** Some adjectives change a lot, others barely change. You need to learn each adjective's forms!

## Slide 9: Comparative Adjectives — Bigger, Smaller, Better

**Comparative adjectives compare TWO things:**

In Somali, comparatives use the pattern: **Noun + adjective + "ka" + other noun**

```
Nin-ka nin-kaas ka weyn. 
(The man [is] than that man big.)
= The man is bigger than that man.

Gabar-ta gabar-taas ka yar.
(The girl [is] than that girl small.)
= The girl is smaller than that girl.

Magaalo-ta magaalo-taas ka weyn.
(The city [is] than that city big.)
= The city is bigger than that city.
```

**Pattern:** Object + ka + Adjective = "more adjective than"

3. **Kitaab-ka kitaab-kaas ka cusub** (The book is newer than that book)

**Key:** The order is: Subject + Comparison Target + "ka" + Adjective

## Slide 12: Superlative Adjectives — The Biggest, The Best

**Superlative adjectives express the EXTREME:**

In Somali, you can say:
- "Most big" = ugu weyn (the biggest)
- "Most small" = ugu yar (the smallest)
- "Most good" = ugu wanaagsan (the best)

**Pattern: "ugu" + adjective = the most/the ___est**

```
Nin-ka waa ugu weyn. (The man is the biggest.)
Gabar-ta waa ugu yar. (The girl is the smallest.)
Kitaab-ka waa ugu cusub. (The book is the newest.)
Magaalo-ta waa ugu wanaagsan. (The city is the best.)
```

## Slide 15: Adjective Position — After the Noun

**Remember: In Somali, adjectives come AFTER the noun, not before!**

| English Order | Somali Order | Meaning |
|---------------|--------------|---------|

| new book | kitaab cusub | book new |
| good woman | naag wanaagsan | woman good |

This is opposite of English! Many learners make this mistake:
- ❌ Wrong: "weyn nin" (big man — word order wrong)
- ✅ Right: "nin weyn" (man big — correct order)

## Slide 4: What Are Verbs?

**Verbs are words that EXPRESS ACTIONS or STATES:**

Actions:
- tag (go), socod (walk), cun (eat), ciyaar (play), qor (write)

States:
- joog (be/stay), hayaa (have), waa (is), gayi (arrive)

**In Somali, verbs are incredibly IMPORTANT because:**
1. They carry tense information (past, present, future)
2. They carry aspect information (completed, ongoing)
3. They AGREE with subjects (person, number, gender)
4. They can be modified with EXTENSIONS (causative, passive, etc.)

**Without understanding verbs, you cannot build sentences!**

## Slide 5: The Triliteral Root System

**Somali verbs are built on THREE-CONSONANT ROOTS:**

```
Root: C-C-C (three consonants)
Example: t-g (TAG = to go)
Example: s-c-d (SOCOD = to walk)
Example: c-n (CUN = to eat)
```

**From ONE root, you create many FORMS:**
- Different tenses (past, present, future)
- Different aspects (completed, ongoing)
- Different moods (actual, subjunctive, etc.)
- Different extensions (causative, passive, etc.)

**Example with ROOT t-g (TAG = go):**
- Present: waan tag (I go)
- Past: waan tagay (I went)
- Future: waan tagi doonaa (I will go)
- Imperative: tag! (go!)
- Causative: tagin (make go/send)

One root, MANY possibilities!

## Slide 6: Verb Stems

**A VERB STEM is the basic form of a verb you build from the root:**

**Common stem patterns:**

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Simple (CVC-V) | tag | go |
| Double (CVCC-V) | socod | walk |
| Long (CVVC-V) | caaf | pass |
| Extended (CVC-VCC-V) | ciyaar | play |

**Each stem has its own:**
- Conjugation patterns
- Agreement markers
- Tense formations

You must LEARN individual verb stems — there's no universal formula!

## Slide 9: Verb Classification — What Kind of Action?

**Verbs are classified by what KIND of action they express:**

**TRANSITIVE VERBS:** Action directed AT SOMEONE/SOMETHING
- cun (eat) — eats SOMETHING
- qor (write) — writes SOMETHING
- arki (see) — sees SOMEONE/SOMETHING
- gaystay (hit) — hits SOMEONE

**INTRANSITIVE VERBS:** Action does NOT require an object
- tag (go) — go (alone, no object needed)
- socod (walk) — walk
- joog (stay/be) — stay/be
- ciyaar (play) — play

**STATIVE VERBS:** Express a STATE or CONDITION
- hayaa (have) — state of possession
- waa (is) — state of being
- jira (exist) — state of existing
- yihiin (are) — state of being

## Slide 12: Subject Agreement — Person & Number

**Verbs AGREE with their subjects in PERSON and NUMBER:**

**The SUBJECT can be:**
- **1st person:** ani (I), innaga (we)
- **2nd person:** adiga (you), idinka (you all)
- **3rd person:** isaga (he), iyada (she), iyaga (they)

**Verbs CHANGE to match:**

```

Innaga waannu tag. (We go) — 1st person plural
Idinka waad tag. (You all go) — 2nd person plural
Iyaga way tag. (They go) — 3rd person plural
```

Notice: The SUBJECT pronouns and the VERB both agree!

## Slide 4: Tense vs. Aspect — Understanding the Difference

**TENSE = WHEN the action happens (time location)**
- Past (bagasse) — actions that already happened
- Present (hadda) — actions happening now
- Future (mustaqbal) — actions that will happen

**ASPECT = HOW the action is viewed (completion/duration)**
- Perfective — action is COMPLETED, viewed as a whole
- Imperfective — action is ONGOING, NOT yet completed
- Habitual — action that HAPPENS REGULARLY

**Key difference:**

| Tense | Aspect | Example | Meaning |
|-------|--------|---------|---------|
| Past | Perfective | waxaan cunay | I ate (finished) |
| Past | Imperfective | waxaan cunaya | I was eating (in progress) |
| Present | Habitual | waxaan cuna | I eat (usually) |
| Future | Perfective | waxaan cuni doonaa | I will eat (and finish) |

**They work TOGETHER to create precise meanings!**

## Slide 5: The Three Tenses

**SOMALI HAS THREE MAIN TENSES:**

**1. PRESENT TENSE — Actions now or habitual**
- Formation: Subject + Agreement marker + Verb stem
- Example: ani waan tag (I go/am going)
- No special marker needed

**2. PAST TENSE — Actions completed**
- Formation: Subject + Agreement marker + Verb stem + **-ay/-ey/-ay**
- Example: ani waan tagay (I went)
- Suffix **-ay** marks past

**3. FUTURE TENSE — Actions upcoming**
- Formation: Subject + Agreement marker + Verb stem + **doonaa/doona/doonaan**
- Example: ani waan tagi doonaa (I will go)
- Auxiliary **doonaa** marks future intention

## Slide 6: The Present Tense

**PRESENT TENSE = actions happening now OR actions done regularly**

**Formation: Subject + Agreement Marker + Verb Stem**

```
ani waan tag (I go / I am going)
adiga waad tag (you go)
isagu wuu tag (he goes)
iyada way tag (she goes)
innaga waannu tag (we go)
idinka waad tag (you all go)
iyaga way tag (they go)
```

**Present tense has NO SUFFIX — it's the base verb stem!**

**Uses:**
- Habitual actions: ani waxaan socod (I walk — usually)
- Current state: wiil-ka wuu joog (the boy is staying)
- General truths: dugsi waa meelka walaacka (school is a place of learning)

## Slide 9: The Past Tense

**PAST TENSE = actions already completed**

**Formation: Subject + Agreement Marker + Verb Stem + -ay (or -ey/-ay variants)**

```

```

**Notice: The suffix changes by subject!**

**Suffixes:**
- Singular: -ay/-ey
- Plural: -een/-ayan

**Uses:**
- Completed actions: ani waxaan cunay (I ate)
- Past states: wiil-ka wuu jooga (the boy was there)
- Narrative: markaa buu timid (then he came)

## Slide 10: Past Tense Patterns

**WATCH THE SUFFIX CHANGE with different subjects:**

```
Perfect aspect (completed):
ani waan tagay (I went — finished)
iyada way tagay (she went — finished)
iyaga way tageen (they went — finished)

Notice: -ay (singular), -een (plural)
```

**Important:** The past tense SUFFIX carries information about WHO did the action!

This is why agreement matters!

## Slide 13: The Future Tense

**FUTURE TENSE = actions that will happen (intended or planned)**

**Formation: Subject + Agreement Marker + Verb Stem + doonaa/doona/doonaan**


**Notice: The auxiliary changes by subject!**

**Uses:**
- Future intention: ani waan tagi doonaa (I will go)
- Future plans: buu curin donaa (he will plant)
- Future habitual: way imaaneen doonaan (they will come often)

## Slide 14: Future vs. Habitual Present

|-------|------|---------|---------|
| Present habitual | stem alone | ani waan tag | I go (usually) |
| Future | stem + doonaa | ani waan tagi doonaa | I will go (future) |
| Past habitual | stem + -ay | ani waan tagay | I used to go |

**Subtle but important!**

## Slide 18: The Aspects — How Actions Are Viewed

**ASPECT describes the CHARACTER of the action, not WHEN it happens.**

**SOMALI HAS THREE MAIN ASPECTS:**

**1. PERFECTIVE ASPECT — Action is COMPLETED, viewed as whole**
- Marked by: past tense suffix (-ay)
- Example: ani waan cunay (I ate — finished)
- Meaning: The action is DONE, FINISHED

**2. IMPERFECTIVE ASPECT — Action is ONGOING, NOT completed**
- Marked by: present tense stem (no suffix)
- Example: ani waan cuna (I am eating — in progress)
- Meaning: The action is HAPPENING NOW

**3. HABITUAL ASPECT — Action HAPPENS REGULARLY or repeatedly**
- Marked by: present tense stem
- Example: ani waan cunaa (I eat — usually/regularly)
- Meaning: Repeated, customary action

## Slide 19: Perfective vs. Imperfective — Practical Difference

**These SOUND similar but mean DIFFERENT things:**

```
PERFECTIVE (completed):
Ani waan cunay. (I ate. — finished eating)
The action is OVER.

IMPERFECTIVE (ongoing):
Ani waan cuna. (I am eating. — still eating)
The action is HAPPENING RIGHT NOW.

HABITUAL:
Ani waan cunaa. (I eat. — usually, habitually)
The action is REGULAR, CUSTOMARY.
```

**In Somali, the MARKER tells the listener WHAT IS DONE and WHAT IS ONGOING!**

## Slide 20: Combining Tense + Aspect

**The REAL POWER: Combine tense (when) + aspect (how) for precise meanings:**

```
PAST + PERFECTIVE:
ani waan cunay (I ate — in the past, finished)

PAST + IMPERFECTIVE:
ani waan cunaya (I was eating — in the past, ongoing)

PRESENT + HABITUAL:
ani waan cunaa (I eat — now, habitually)

FUTURE + PERFECTIVE:
ani waan cuni doonaa (I will eat — in the future, will finish)

FUTURE + IMPERFECTIVE:
ani waan cunaaya doona (I will be eating — future, ongoing)
```

**This is how Somali speakers express PRECISE TIME and ACTION NATURE!**

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Moods in Action

**Notice the ATTITUDE change toward the action:**

```
STATEMENT (Indicative — actual fact):
Ani waan tag. (I go. — This is a fact.)

WISH (Optative — desired outcome):
Waxaan doon tag! (I want to go! — Expressing desire)

COMMAND (Imperative — direct order):
Tag! (Go! — Telling someone to do it)

CONDITION (Conditional — hypothetical):
Hadii aad tag, buu imaan. (If you go, he will come. — Possibility)

PURPOSE (Subjunctive — why):
Waxaan tag inaan joogaa. (I go so that I might stay. — Expressing goal)
```

Notice: SAME VERB ROOT (tag) but DIFFERENT MOODS express different attitudes!

## Slide 4: What Is Mood?

**MOOD = the ATTITUDE or RELATIONSHIP toward an action**

**Four questions mood answers:**
1. **Is it REAL?** (Indicative mood)
2. **Is it a COMMAND?** (Imperative mood)
3. **Is it POSSIBLE or CONDITIONAL?** (Conditional mood)
4. **Is it DESIRED or NECESSARY?** (Subjunctive/Optative mood)

**Compare:**
- "I eat." (Indicative — real fact)
- "Eat!" (Imperative — command)
- "If I eat, I'll be full." (Conditional — possibility)
- "I want to eat!" (Optative — desire)

**Each mood has different FORMS and FUNCTIONS!**

## Slide 5: The Indicative Mood (Reality)

**INDICATIVE MOOD = statements about WHAT IS REAL, ACTUAL, TRUE**

**This is what you've been learning so far!**

```
Present:     Ani waan tag. (I go.)
Past:        Ani waan tagay. (I went.)
Future:      Ani waan tagi doonaa. (I will go.)
```

**Uses:**
- Facts: Luuqa Soomaali waa luuqa afrikaanka. (Somali is an African language.)
- Events: Marka buu timid. (When he came...)
- Descriptions: Gabar-ta way qurux. (The girl is beautiful.)

**The indicative mood DOES NOT CHANGE FORM — it's what you already know!**

## Slide 6: The Imperative Mood (Commands)

**IMPERATIVE MOOD = direct commands or instructions**

**Formation: VERB STEM ALONE (no subject pronoun, no agreement marker)**

```
Tag! (Go! — singular)
Socda! (Walk! — singular)
Cun! (Eat! — singular)

Tagna! (Go! — plural, softly)
Socda! (Walk! — plural)
Cunta! (Eat! — plural)
```

**Important:** Imperatives are COMMANDS, so no subject needed — the listener IS the subject!

**Uses:**
- Direct orders: Tag hadda! (Go now!)
- Instructions: Qor magacaaga. (Write your name.)
- Requests: Cab bilow. (Give me water.)
- Invitations: Cunaado! (Eat with us!)

## Slide 7: Forming Singular Imperatives

**For SINGULAR commands (talking to ONE person):**

**Formation: Just the VERB STEM — nothing else needed**

```
tag (go!)
socod (walk!)
cun (eat!)
arki (see!)
qor (write!)
ciyaar (play!)
```

**Examples in sentences:**
- Ani tag! (You, go!)
- Caruurta socda! (Children, walk!)
- Bilow cun! (Begin, eat!)

**That's it — imperatives are SIMPLE!**

## Slide 8: Forming Plural Imperatives

**For PLURAL commands (talking to MULTIPLE people):**

**Formation: VERB STEM + -a (or -ta) for plural)**

```
tagna (go! — plural)
socda (walk! — plural)
cunta (eat! — plural)
arkida (see! — plural)
qorta (write! — plural)
```

**Examples:**
- Tagna hadda! (You all, go now!)
- Cunta caasaladig. (Eat the rice.)
- Socda walaalo! (Walk, brothers!)

## Slide 11: The Subjunctive Mood (Desire, Necessity, Purpose)

**SUBJUNCTIVE MOOD = expressing:**
- Wishes ("I hope that...")
- Necessity ("It is necessary that...")
- Purpose ("in order that...")
- Goals ("so that...")

**Formation: Subject + Agreement Marker + VERB STEM + -o (or -aa)**

```
Waxaan doon (inaan) tago. (I want to go.)
Waad baahan tahay (inaad) tago. (You need to go.)
Wuu rabo (inuu) tago. (He wants to go.)
Way raba (inay) tago. (She wants to go.)
```

**Notice: The -o suffix marks SUBJUNCTIVE!**

**Uses:**
- Desires: Waxaan doon inaan tag. (I want to go.)
- Necessity: Waad baahan tahay inaad socdo. (You must walk.)
- Purpose: Buu tag inaan cumri lagu jiro. (He left so that I might live.)
- Goals: Way tag inay walaalnimada dhaafso. (She goes to escape sisterhood.)

## Slide 12: Subjunctive vs. Indicative

**Compare the DIFFERENCE between indicative and subjunctive:**

| Subjunctive | tago | ani waxaan doon inaan tago | I want to go (desire) |
| Indicative | tag | ani waan tag | I go (present) |
| Subjunctive | tago | waad baahan tahay inaad tago | you must go (necessity) |

**The SUBJUNCTIVE shifts from FACT to DESIRE/NECESSITY!**

## Slide 15: The Conditional Mood (If-Then)

**CONDITIONAL MOOD = expressing HYPOTHETICAL or POSSIBLE situations**

**Formation: Hadii (if) + present + past or future**

```
Hadii aad tag, buu imaan. 
(If you go, he will come. — possibility)

Hadii aad socdo, buu ku arkan.
(If you walk, he will see you. — condition)

Hadii uu cunay, wuu buuxdan.
(If he ate, he would be full. — past condition)
```

**Structure:**
1. **Condition clause:** Hadii (if) + subject + present or past tense
2. **Result clause:** Subject + future or past + rest

**Uses:**
- Possibilities: Hadii aad tagi lahayd, lagu arkan lahayd. (If you could go, you would be seen.)
- Warnings: Hadii aad tag, abti baa kukulan. (If you go, uncle will meet you.)
- Explanations: Hadii uu jiro, buu la ciyaari lahayd. (If he existed, you would play with him.)

## Slide 16: Conditional Patterns

**CONDITIONAL structures use PAIRED TENSES:**

```
POSSIBILITY (present condition → future result):
Hadii aad tag, buu imaan.
(If you go now, he will come.)

PAST CONDITION (past condition → past result):
Hadii aad tagay, wuu imaan.
(If you went, he came.)

HYPOTHETICAL (impossible condition):
Hadii aad tagi lahayd, buu ku arkan lahayd.
(If you could go, he would see you.)
```

**The TENSE in both clauses shows the RELATIONSHIP!**

## Slide 19: The Optative Mood (Wishes & Hopes)

**OPTATIVE MOOD = expressing WISHES, HOPES, and DESIRES for outcomes**

**Uses:**
- Blessings: Exa ummadda Soomaali guud! (May all of Somalia!)
- Hopes: Illaahi cid kasta ay caafi tahay! (God willing, everyone is well!)
- Wishes: Waxaan rabtaa inaan guriga jooga! (I wish I were home!)

| Mood | Purpose | Form | Example |
|------|---------|------|---------|
| **Indicative** | Express facts | Normal tense markers | Ani waan tag. (I go.) |
| **Imperative** | Give commands | Bare stem | Tag! (Go!) |
| **Conditional** | Express possibilities | Hadii + paired tenses | Hadii aad tag, buu imaan. |
| **Subjunctive** | Express desires, necessity | Stem + -o | Waxaan doon inaan tago. |
| **Optative** | Express wishes, hopes | Special expressions | Exa aad caafi! (Be well!) |

**Moods are how you express ATTITUDES and RELATIONSHIPS to actions!**

## Slide 23: Mood vs. Tense vs. Aspect

**Don't confuse these three grammar systems:**

```
TENSE = WHEN (past, present, future)
Ani waan tagay. (I went — past tense)

ASPECT = HOW COMPLETED (perfective, imperfective, habitual)
Ani waan cunay. (I ate — perfective aspect)

MOOD = ATTITUDE (indicative, imperative, conditional, subjunctive, optative)
Tag! (Go! — imperative mood)
```

**They work TOGETHER to create complete meaning!**

Example with all three:
- **Ani waan tagay inaan cureen.** 
- Tense: PAST (tagay)
- Aspect: PERFECTIVE (action completed)
- Mood: SUBJUNCTIVE (purpose clause)
- Meaning: "I went in order to work" (past fact + completed action + expressed purpose)

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Verb Extensions in Action

**Notice how ONE VERB ROOT appears in DIFFERENT FORMS:**

```
BASIC VERB:
Ani waan tag. (I go.)
tag = basic verb, I am the DOER

CAUSATIVE (make someone go):
Ani waan tagin. (I make/send.)

```

## Slide 4: What Are Verb Extensions?

**VERB EXTENSIONS are MODIFICATIONS to a verb that change its MEANING or RELATIONSHIP**

**Extensions answer these questions:**
1. **Who is affected?** (Causative: someone else; Reflexive: myself)
2. **Who is the focus?** (Passive: the receiver; Active: the doer)
3. **How is it done?** (Iterative: repeatedly; Intensive: strongly)
4. **Who benefits?** (Applicative: someone benefits)

**Somali uses PREFIXES and SUFFIXES to create extensions:**

```
Prefix: la- (marks passive and applicative)
Prefix: is- (marks reflexive)
Suffix: -in or -i (marks causative)
Suffix: -aa (marks intensive)
```

## Slide 5: The Causative Extension (Make/Cause)

**CAUSATIVE = making or causing someone to do something**

**Formation: VERB STEM + -in (causative suffix)**

```
Basic:     tag (go)
Causative: tagin (make/send go)

Basic:     cun (eat)
Causative: cunin (make eat, feed)

Basic:     socod (walk)
Causative: socdin (make walk)

Basic:     joog (stay)
Causative: joogin (make stay)
```

**In sentences:**
- Ani waan tagin. (I make go / I send)
- Wuu cunin. (He feeds / he makes eat)
- Way socdin. (She makes walk)

**Uses:**
- Cause an action: Ani waan tagin gabar-ta. (I send the girl.)
- Make someone do something: Wuu cunin caruurta. (He feeds the children.)
- Express delegation: Buu qorin macalimka. (He makes the teacher write.)

## Slide 6: Causative Examples in Context

**Causative verbs FOCUS on the person DOING THE CAUSING:**

```
ACTIVE (without causative):
Wiil-ka wuu tag. (The boy goes.)
— The boy is the DOER.

CAUSATIVE (with causative):
Gabar-ka way tagin wiil-ka. (The girl makes/sends the boy.)
— The girl CAUSES the boy to go.

ACTIVE:
Caruurta way cuna. (The children eat.)
— Children are the DOERS.

CAUSATIVE:
Hooyo way cunin caruurta. (Mother feeds the children.)
— Mother CAUSES the children to eat.
```

**The causative EXTENDS the verb to express a NEW relationship!**

## Slide 9: The Passive Voice (Focus on Receiver)

**PASSIVE VOICE = focusing on WHO/WHAT RECEIVES the action, NOT who does it**

**Formation: Subject + la- prefix + VERB STEM**

```
ACTIVE (focus on doer):
Wiil-ka wuu cunay caruur. (The boy ate food.)
— WHO ate? The boy.

PASSIVE (focus on receiver):
Caruur-ka waa la cunay. (The food was eaten.)
— WHAT was eaten? The food.

ACTIVE:
Gabar-ta way arkisay ninka. (The girl saw the man.)

PASSIVE:
Ninka waa la arkisay. (The man was seen.)
— Focus on the man, not the girl.
```

**The la- prefix marks passive!**

## Slide 10: Passive Voice in Context

**PASSIVE focuses the LISTENER'S ATTENTION on the RECEIVER:**

```
ACTIVE SENTENCES (focus on DOER):
- Macalimka wuu qoray xariif. (The teacher wrote the letter.)
  Focus: Teacher (DOER)

PASSIVE SENTENCES (focus on RECEIVER):
- Xariifka waa la qoray. (The letter was written.)
  Focus: Letter (RECEIVER)

Why use passive?
1. The doer is UNKNOWN: Magaalada waa la durmay. (The city was destroyed.)
2. The doer is OBVIOUS: Baarka waa la dhammaysay. (The work was completed.)
3. The receiver is MORE IMPORTANT: Dugsiga waa la dhamaystay. (The school was finished.)
```

**Passive shifts FOCUS and EMPHASIS!**

## Slide 13: The Reflexive Extension (To Oneself)

**REFLEXIVE = action directed TOWARD ONESELF or DONE BY ONESELF**

**Formation: Subject + is- prefix + VERB STEM**

```
ACTIVE:
Ani waan caashi garuur. (I wash the child.)
— I wash SOMEONE ELSE.

REFLEXIVE:
Ani waan is caashi. (I wash myself.)
— I wash MY OWN SELF.

ACTIVE:
Wuu barbar qor. (He teaches the people.)

REFLEXIVE:
Wuu is bar. (He teaches himself.)
— Self-education, learning on one's own.
```

**The is- prefix marks reflexive!**

## Slide 14: Reflexive Examples

**REFLEXIVE actions are personal or independent:**

```
ACTIVE (doing to others):
Gabar-ta way barayed caruurta. (The girl taught the children.)

REFLEXIVE (doing to self):
Gabar-ta way is baraysay. (The girl taught herself.)

ACTIVE:
Ninka wuu qoray mahadsantii. (The man wrote the thank-you.)

REFLEXIVE:
Ninka wuu is qoray. (The man wrote for himself / self-wrote.)

Uses:
- Personal action: waan is dhaqay (I washed myself)
- Independent learning: way is baratay (she taught herself)
- Self-care: buu is carro (he braided his hair)
```

## Slide 17: The Iterative/Repetitive Extension (Keep Doing)

**ITERATIVE = action done REPEATEDLY, CONTINUOUSLY, or HABITUALLY**

**Formation: VERB ROOT + VERB ROOT (doubling) or special suffixes**

```
SIMPLE (one action):
Ani waan socod. (I walk. — one instance)

ITERATIVE (repeated):
Ani waan socod-socod. (I walk-walk. — repeatedly/continuously)

SIMPLE:
Gabar-ta way cun. (The girl eats. — one meal)

ITERATIVE:
Gabar-ta way cun-cun. (The girl eats-eats. — keeps eating)
```

**ROOT DOUBLING marks iterative/habitual repetition!**

## Slide 18: Iterative in Context

```

ITERATIVE (repeated action):
Buu socod-socod. (He walks-walks. — habitually, keeps walking)

SIMPLE:
Wuu ciyaar. (He plays. — one time)

ITERATIVE:
Wuu ciyaar-ciyaar. (He plays-plays. — keeps playing, repeatedly)

Uses:
- Continuous action: waan tag-tag (I keep going)
- Habitual: way cun-cun (she keeps eating)
- Ongoing state: buu joog-joog (he stays-stays, remains)
```

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Irregularities in Action

**Notice the UNUSUAL PATTERNS:**

```
REGULAR VERB (what you've learned):
Ani waan tag. (I go.)
Ani waan tagay. (I went.)
Ani waan tagi doonaa. (I will go.)

IRREGULAR VERB (imid = come):
Ani waan imid. (I came. — Note: imid, not imdaa)
Buu timid. (He came. — Note: timid, not tamdaa)
Way imaan. (She will come. — Note: imaan, not imid doona)

Why irregular?
The verb "come" changes ROOT depending on TENSE!
Past: imid (I came)
Future: imaan (I will come)
It's NOT just adding markers—it's changing the BASE!
```

Notice: Irregular verbs are COMMON and IMPORTANT!

## Slide 4: What Makes a Verb Irregular?

**An IRREGULAR VERB breaks the normal conjugation patterns.**

**Typical patterns you expect (regular verbs):**
- Same root in all tenses
- Predictable suffixes (-ay for past, doonaa for future)
- Predictable agreement markers

**What makes a verb IRREGULAR:**
1. **Changes the root stem** (imid vs. timid vs. imaan — same verb, different roots!)
2. **Skips normal suffixes** (buu timid = came, not buu tim-ay)
3. **Has unique agreement** (different markers than normal verbs)
4. **Combines unpredictably** (doesn't follow extension rules)

**Result: You can't PREDICT the form — you must LEARN IT!**

## Slide 5: Major Irregular Verbs

**SOMALI HAS SEVERAL HIGHLY IRREGULAR BUT VERY COMMON VERBS:**

| Infinitive | Meaning | Present | Past | Future | Pattern |
|-----------|---------|---------|------|--------|---------|
| imid | come | imad/imaa | imid/timid | imaan | SUPPLETIVE |
| jid | go/depart | jid | jid/jidid | jidi/jidan | SEMI-REGULAR |
| ahaa | be | ahaa | ahaa | noqon | HIGHLY IRREGULAR |
| fikir | think | fikir | fikiriye | fikir doona | MOSTLY REGULAR |

**These are the MOST common irregular verbs in Somali!**

## Slide 6: The Verb IMID (Come) — Highly Irregular

**IMID is one of the most irregular verbs in Somali.**

**Formation by tense:**

```
PRESENT (habitual/general):
Ani waan imaa. (I come. — habitual)
Isagu wuu imaa. (He comes.)
Iyada way imaa. (She comes.)

PAST (completed action):
Ani waan imid. (I came. — not "imay"!)
Isagu wuu timid. (He came. — uses "timid," not "imid"!)
Iyada way imid. (She came.)
Iyaga way imeen. (They came. — plural)

FUTURE (intended action):
Ani waan imaan. (I will come. — not "iman doonaa"!)
Isagu wuu imaan. (He will come.)
Iyada way imaan. (She will come.)
```

**Notice: Three different roots for three different tenses!**

## Slide 7: IMID Conjugation Patterns

**Why is IMID so irregular?**

```
Most verbs work like TAG:
Present: tag
Past:    tagay (add -ay)
Future:  tagi doonaa (add doonaa)

IMID breaks this pattern:
Present: imaa (different root)
Past:    imid (different root again)
Future:  imaan (yet another root!)

It's almost like three different verbs!
```

**This is SUPPLETIVE — using different verb roots for different tenses!**

## Slide 10: The Verb AHAA (Be) — Stative Irregular

**AHAA (be/exist) is another highly irregular verb.**

```
PRESENT:
Ani waan ahaa. (I am.)
Isagu wuu ahaa. (He is.)

PAST:
Ani waan ahaa. (I was. — Same form as present!)
Isagu wuu ahaa. (He was.)

FUTURE:
Ani waan noqon doonaa. (I will be. — Uses different root: noqon!)
Isagu wuu noqon donaa. (He will become.)
```

**AHAA has an unusual feature: Present and past use SAME FORM!**

The future uses a completely different verb (noqon = become)!

## Slide 11: AHAA Usage Examples

**AHAA is used for describing states and characteristics:**

```
PRESENT/PAST (no clear distinction):
Gabar-ta way ahaa macallima. (The girl is/was a teacher.)
Ninka wuu ahaa cariim. (The man is/was strong.)
Waxani waa roon. (This is wrong. — state)

FUTURE (using NOQON):
Gabar-ta way noqon doontaa macallima. (The girl will become a teacher.)
Ninka wuu noqon donaa cariim. (The man will become strong.)
```

**AHAA + NOQON combination is how Somali expresses being/becoming!**

## Slide 14: Gemination (Double Consonants)

**GEMINATION = doubling a consonant to change verb meaning or intensity**

**Pattern:**
- Single consonant: normal action
- Double consonant: intensified, repeated, or forceful action

**Examples:**

```
SINGLE:
waxaan qor (I write — normal)
waxaan socod (I walk — normal)
waxaan cun (I eat — normal)

DOUBLE (intensified/repeated):
waxaan qooor (I write intensely / keep writing)
waxaan soocod (I walk forcefully / keep walking)
waxaan cuun (I eat greedily / keep eating)
```

**Gemination adds EMPHASIS or INTENSITY!**

## Slide 15: Gemination Examples

**Double consonants create SUBTLE MEANING SHIFTS:**

```
NORMAL:
Tag hadda! (Go now. — normal command)

INTENSIFIED:
Taag hadda! (Go right now! — urgent, emphatic)

NORMAL:
Wuu joog. (He stays. — regular)

INTENSIFIED:
Wuu jooj! (He stays put! / He stops! — emphatic)

NORMAL:
Way cun. (She eats. — regular)

INTENSIFIED:
Way cuun. (She eats greedily! — emphasizes eating)
```

**Gemination = INTENSITY, EMPHASIS, or REPETITION!**

## Slide 18: Suppletive Verbs (Different Roots)

**SUPPLETIVE VERBS use DIFFERENT ROOTS for different tenses.**

**Most famous example: TAG (go) and JIDA/JID (depart)**

```
For "going":
TAG (go):     ani waan tag (I go), ani waan tagay (I went)
JID (depart): buu jid (he departed), way jiden (they departed)

These are SEPARATE verbs with DIFFERENT shades of meaning!

TAG = general going
JID = departing, setting out with purpose
```

**Somali sometimes uses different verbs to express fine distinctions!**

## Slide 19: Common Suppletive Distinctions

|-------------|----------|---------|---------|

| Arriving (purpose achieved) | gaari | way gaari | she arrived |
| Staying (general) | joog | wuu joog | he stays |
| Remaining (lingering) | hadhaw | way hadhaw | she lingers |

**Suppletive forms give NUANCE to expressions!**

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Word Order in Action

**Notice how WORD ORDER changes MEANING and EMPHASIS:**

```
STANDARD SVO (Subject-Verb-Object):
Gabar-ta way cunay cambuula.
(The girl ate the food.)
Focus: What happened? The girl ate food.

VSO (Verb-Subject-Object) — EMPHASIS:
Way cunay gabar-ta cambuula.
(The food ATE the girl. — focus on the eating, unusual)

VSO with FOCUS MARKER (baa):
Cambuula baa gabar-ta cunaysay.
(FOOD is what the girl was eating. — emphasizing the food)

OBJECT-FIRST (marked by baa):

(It is the FOOD that the girl ate. — fronting the object)
```

Notice: SAME WORDS, DIFFERENT ORDER = DIFFERENT MEANING!

## Slide 4: What Is Word Order?

**WORD ORDER = the sequence in which subject, verb, and object appear in a sentence**

**Three main patterns exist in world languages:**
- **SVO:** Subject-Verb-Object (English: "I eat food")
- **SOV:** Subject-Object-Verb (Japanese: "I food eat")
- **VSO:** Verb-Subject-Object (Irish: "Ate I food")

**Somali is PRIMARILY SVO but allows VSO with special meaning!**

**Basic structure:**

```
Subject + Verb + Object = Simple sentence

Ani + waan cun + cambuula.
(I + eat + food.)
S  + V      + O
```

## Slide 5: The SVO Pattern (Subject-Verb-Object)

**SVO is the NEUTRAL, NORMAL word order in Somali.**

**This is what you've been building so far:**

```
Subject (S) + Verb (V) + Object (O)

Ani waan cun cambuula. (I eat food.)
S   V       O

Gabar-ta way arkis ninka. (The girl sees the man.)
S        V      O

Wiil-ka wuu qor xariif. (The boy writes a letter.)
S      V    O
```

**SVO is NEUTRAL — no special emphasis.**

**Uses:**
- Reporting facts: Macalimka wuu baro dugsi. (The teacher teaches school.)
- Describing actions: Ninka wuu socod. (The man walks.)
- Answering "what happened?": Iyada way cunay. (She ate.)

4. **Iyaga way cun cuntada.** (They eat the food.)

## Slide 8: The VSO Pattern (Verb-Subject-Object)

**VSO is used when you want to EMPHASIZE the ACTION or CHANGE FOCUS.**

**Formation: Verb + Subject + Object**

```
NORMAL (SVO):
Gabar-ta way cunay cuntada.
(The girl ate the food.)
Focus: Neutral statement

EMPHATIC (VSO):
Way cunay gabar-ta cuntada.
(ATE the girl the food. — emphasizing the eating action)
Focus: The action itself

EXTREME EMPHASIS:
Cunay! Gabar-ta cunay cuntada!
(ATE! The girl ate the food! — very emphatic)
```

**VSO shifts focus to the VERB/ACTION!**

## Slide 9: VSO in Context

**VSO is used in STORYTELLING, DRAMA, and EMOTIONAL CONTEXTS:**

```
NEUTRAL NARRATIVE (SVO):
Gabar-ta way socdatay suuqa.
(The girl walked to the market.)

VIVID STORYTELLING (VSO):
Way socdatay gabar-ta suuqa!
(WALKED the girl to the market! — dramatic)

NEUTRAL:
Ninka wuu timid markay arkis.
(The man came when she saw.)

EMPHATIC:
Timid! Ninka timid markay arkis!
(HE CAME! The man came when she saw! — drama)
```

**VSO creates ENERGY and EMPHASIS!**

## Slide 12: Focus Marker BAA — The Game Changer

**The focus marker BAA is how Somali signals what is MOST IMPORTANT in a sentence.**

**BAA has multiple uses:**

```
Use 1: HIGHLIGHT THE SUBJECT
Gabar-ta baa way cunaysay.
(The GIRL (not someone else) was eating. — focus on subject)

Use 2: HIGHLIGHT THE OBJECT
Cuntada baa gabar-ta cunaysay.
(FOOD (not something else) is what the girl ate. — focus on object)

Use 3: HIGHLIGHT AN ADVERBIAL
Suuqa baa way socdatay.
(To the MARKET (not elsewhere) the girl walked. — focus on location)

Use 4: HIGHLIGHT A PREDICATE
Qurux baa gabar-ta.
(BEAUTIFUL the girl is. — focus on the quality)
```

**BAA moves the focused element to the FRONT of the sentence!**

## Slide 13: BAA Structure and Rules

**BAA creates CLEFT SENTENCES (focusing one element):**

**Formation: [FOCUSED ELEMENT] baa [REST OF SENTENCE]**

```
Normal (SVO):
Gabar-ta way cunay cuntada.
(The girl ate the food.)

With BAA (focusing subject):
Gabar-ta baa way cunay cuntada.
(It is the GIRL who ate the food.)

With BAA (focusing object):
Cuntada baa gabar-ta cunaysay.
(It is the FOOD the girl was eating.)

With BAA (focusing time):
Mooday baa ay socdeen.
(It was YESTERDAY they walked.)
```

**BAA lets you emphasize ANY element!**

## Slide 16: Adjuncts — Adding Extra Information

**Adjuncts are OPTIONAL phrases that add TIME, PLACE, or MANNER information.**

**Adjuncts usually go at the END of the sentence:**

```
Basic: Gabar-ta way cunay.
(The girl ate.)

+ Time: Gabar-ta way cunay marka dhexe.
(The girl ate at noon.)

+ Place: Gabar-ta way cunay guriga.
(The girl ate at home.)

+ Manner: Gabar-ta way cunay si deg-deg.
(The girl ate quickly.)

+ Multiple: Gabar-ta way cunay guriga mara dhexe.
(The girl ate at home at noon.)
```

**Adjuncts provide CONTEXT without changing the core meaning!**

## Slide 17: Adjunct Types

**ADJUNCTS answer the questions: WHERE? WHEN? HOW? WHY? WITH WHOM?**

| Type | Question | Example | Sentence |
|------|----------|---------|----------|

| **Comitative** | With whom? | walaalo (brothers), macalimka (teacher) | Way socdeen walaalo. (They walked with brothers.) |

**Each adjunct adds RICHNESS to the sentence!**

   b) Gabar-ta baa way cunay.
   c) Way cunay gabar-ta.

## Slide 26: Exercise 6 Answers

1. "Emphasize GIRL (not someone else)." → **b) Gabar-ta baa way cunay.**
2. "Emphasize the ACTION of eating." → **c) Way cunay gabar-ta.**
3. "State as neutral fact." → **a) Gabar-ta way cunay.**

**Key:** Word order carries COMMUNICATIVE MEANING!

## Slide 27: Summary — Word Order & Simple Sentences

✅ **SVO (NEUTRAL):** Subject-Verb-Object is the default word order
- Gabar-ta way cunay cuntada. (The girl ate the food.)

✅ **VSO (EMPHATIC):** Verb-Subject-Object emphasizes the action
- Way cunay gabar-ta cuntada. (The girl ATE the food!)

✅ **BAA (FOCUS):** Moves focused element to front
- Cuntada baa gabar-ta cunaysay. (It's the FOOD she ate.)

✅ **ADJUNCTS:** Optional phrases for time, place, manner
- Way cunay guriga mara dhexe. (She ate at home at noon.)

✅ **TEMPLATE:** [Subject] + [Agreement] + [Verb] + [Object] + [Adjuncts]

Word order is how Somali speakers CONTROL MEANING and EMPHASIS!

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Complex Sentences in Action

**Notice how sentences COMBINE and RELATE to each other:**

```
TWO SIMPLE SENTENCES:
Ani waan tag iskuulka. (I go to school.)
Macalimka wuu baro. (The teacher teaches.)

COORDINATED (both ideas are equal):

(I go to school and the teacher teaches.)

SUBORDINATED (one idea depends on the other):
Markii ani aad tag iskuulka, macalimka baa baro.
(When I go to school, the teacher teaches.)

WITH RELATIVE CLAUSE (describing a noun):

(The teacher who teaches school is admirable.)

(When I went to school where the teacher teaches, she ate.)
```

Notice: COMBINING SENTENCES creates TEXTURE and MEANING!

## Slide 4: What Are Complex Sentences?

**A COMPLEX SENTENCE combines TWO or MORE simple sentences or clauses.**

**Three main ways to combine:**

1. **COORDINATION:** Join sentences as EQUALS
   - "I go AND you stay"
   - Sentences of EQUAL importance

2. **SUBORDINATION:** One clause DEPENDS on another
   - "WHEN I go, you stay"
   - One clause is DEPENDENT on the other

3. **RELATIVE CLAUSES:** Describe or define a noun
   - "The teacher WHO teaches is good"
   - Extra information about a noun

**Each strategy has DIFFERENT CONNECTORS and WORD ORDERS!**

## Slide 5: Coordination — Joining Equal Ideas

**COORDINATION connects sentences of EQUAL importance using CONNECTORS.**

**Main Somali coordinators:**

```
OO = and (links two complete thoughts)
LAAKIIN = but (contrasts two ideas)
HADII KALE = otherwise (provides alternative)
AMI = or (offers choice)

Examples:
Ani waan tag OO adiga waad joog.
(I go AND you stay. — two equal actions)

Gabar-ta way cunay cuntada LAAKIIN wiil-ka ma cunin.
(The girl ate the food BUT the boy didn't eat. — contrast)

Way socdeen iskuulka HADII KALE guriga.
(They went to school OTHERWISE home. — alternative)

Waxaan doon tag iskuulka AMI suuqa.
(I want to go to school OR market. — choice)
```

**Coordinators join EQUAL clauses!**

## Slide 6: Coordination Patterns

**COORDINATION doesn't change WORD ORDER — just adds a connector:**

```
Simple 1: Gabar-ta way cunay.
Simple 2: Wiil-ka wuu socdaa.

Coordinated:
Gabar-ta way cunay OO wiil-ka wuu socdaa.
(The girl ate AND the boy walks. — both unchanged)

Gabar-ta way cunay LAAKIIN wiil-ka ma cunin.
(The girl ate BUT the boy didn't eat. — equal clauses)
```

**Each clause keeps its OWN SUBJECT and VERB!**

**Key:** Coordinators join EQUAL, INDEPENDENT clauses!

## Slide 9: Subordination — One Clause Depends on Another

**SUBORDINATION makes one clause DEPENDENT on another using SUBORDINATORS.**

**Main Somali subordinators:**

```
MARKII = when (time)
HADII = if (condition)
SABABTOO AH = because (reason)
INAAN/INAAD/ETC. = that (purpose/result)
INTAAD = while (duration)

Examples:
MARKII ani aad tag, way imaan.
(WHEN I go, she will come. — time dependent on action)

HADII aad tag, buu imaan.
(IF you go, he will come. — result depends on condition)

SABABTOO AH way caafi, way socdeen.
(BECAUSE she was well, they walked. — reason for action)

Way tag INAAN cunto.
(She goes TO EAT. — purpose depends on going)
```

**Subordinators create DEPENDENCY relationships!**

## Slide 10: Main vs. Dependent Clauses

**In subordination, you have TWO clauses:**

1. **MAIN CLAUSE:** The primary idea (can stand alone)
   - Example: Way imaan. (She will come.)

2. **DEPENDENT CLAUSE:** Adds information, CAN'T stand alone
   - Example: Markii aad tag. (When you go... — incomplete!)

**Together:**

```
DEPENDENT CLAUSE + MAIN CLAUSE:
Markii aad tag + way imaan
When you go + she will come
(Complete thought: "When you go, she will come")

MAIN CLAUSE + DEPENDENT CLAUSE:
Way imaan + markii aad tag
She will come + when you go
(Same meaning, different order)
```

**The main clause is the "heart"; dependent clauses ADD information!**

## Slide 13: Relative Clauses — Describing Nouns

**A RELATIVE CLAUSE adds information about a NOUN.**

**Pattern: [NOUN] + [RELATIVE MARKER] + [DESCRIPTIVE CLAUSE]**

```
Basic noun: Gabar-ta
(The girl)

With relative clause:
Gabar-ta oo qurux
(The girl who is beautiful)

More complete:
Gabar-ta oo qurux ama way tag iskuulka
(The girl who is beautiful and goes to school)

In context:
Gabar-ta oo qurux baa waxaan arki.
(The girl WHO IS BEAUTIFUL is what I see.)
```

**Relative clauses describe WHICH ONE or ADD DETAILS about nouns!**

## Slide 14: Relative Clause Markers

**Somali uses specific markers for relative clauses:**

```
OO = who/which (most common)

Examples:
Ninka oo qor xariif. (The man who writes letters.)
Dugsi-ga oo wara caruur. (The school which has children.)
Cuntada oo caasaladig. (The food which is rice.)

TAAS OO = that (which)

Example:
Taas oo macalimmada aad macquul yahay.
(That (which) the teachers (are) admirable.)

KAAS OO = the one who

Example:
Kaas oo tag aah inuu macalim. (The one who goes is a teacher.)
```

**OO is the most common relative marker!**

## Slide 17: Embedding Clauses — Multiple Levels

**EMBEDDED CLAUSES nest SMALLER clauses INSIDE LARGER ones.**

**Simple example:**

```
Level 1 (Main): Way imaan.
(She will come.)

Level 2 (add when): Markii aad tag, way imaan.
(When you go, she will come.)

Level 3 (add where): Markii aad tag iskuulka, way imaan.
(When you go to school, she will come.)

Level 4 (add who): Markii aad tag iskuulka oo macalim jira, way imaan.
(When you go to school where there is a teacher, she will come.)
```

**Each embedding adds a LAYER OF MEANING!**

## Slide 18: Complex Embedding Example

**Real Somali has RICH EMBEDDING:**

```
Gabar-ta oo qurux, markii ay tag iskuulka oo waxbar jira,

(The girl who was beautiful, when she went to school where teaching happens,
she ate the food which was rice, because her body needed it.)

BREAKDOWN:
- Main: way cunay (she ate)
- Relative on "gabar": oo qurux (who is beautiful)
- Time: markii ay tag (when she went)
- Locative: iskuulka oo waxbar jira (school where teaching is)
- Object: cuntada oo caasaladig (food which is rice)
- Reason: sababtoo ah way jidhihaydii raajay (because her body needed)
```

**This is SOPHISTICATED, LAYERED Somali!**

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Negation in Action

**Notice how negation CHANGES the sentence:**

```
POSITIVE:
Ani waan tag. (I go.)

NEGATIVE:
Ani ma tag. (I don't go.)

POSITIVE:
Gabar-ta way cunay cuntada. (The girl ate the food.)

NEGATIVE:
Gabar-ta ma cunin cuntada. (The girl didn't eat the food.)

POSITIVE:
Wuu imaan. (He will come.)

NEGATIVE:
Ma imaan. (He won't come. — completely different form!)

POSITIVE:
Jira cida. (There is something.)

NEGATIVE:
Ma jira cidina. (There is nothing. — with indefinite))
```

Notice: Negation TRANSFORMS the verb form!

## Slide 4: The Negation Particle MA

**MA is the primary NEGATION PARTICLE in Somali.**

**Key properties of MA:**
1. Goes BEFORE the verb
2. REMOVES the agreement marker (waan → ma, wuu → ma, way → ma)
3. Changes the VERB FORM (often using infinitive or special forms)
4. Works with ALL tenses

**Basic pattern:**

```
POSITIVE:    Subject + [Agreement] + Verb
Ani waan tag.      (I go.)

NEGATIVE:    Subject + ma + [Infinitive or special form]
Ani ma tag.        (I don't go.)
```

**MA is like an ERASER for the agreement marker!**

## Slide 5: Present Tense Negation

**PRESENT NEGATION uses the infinitive form:**

```
POSITIVE:
Ani waan tag. (I go.)
Isagu wuu arki. (He sees.)
Way cun. (She eats.)

NEGATIVE:
Ani ma tag. (I don't go. — infinitive)
Isagu ma arki. (He doesn't see. — infinitive)
Way cunin. (She doesn't eat. — infinitive)

Notice: The MA-form uses the infinitive, NOT the +agreement form!
```

**Present negation: subject + ma + INFINITIVE**

## Slide 6: Past Tense Negation

**PAST NEGATION uses a DIFFERENT verb form (often infinitive or special past negative):**

```
POSITIVE (past):
Ani waan tagay. (I went.)
Gabar-ta way cunay. (The girl ate.)
Wiil-ka wuu arkay. (The boy saw.)

NEGATIVE (past):
Ani ma tagin. (I didn't go. — special form)
Gabar-ta ma cunin. (The girl didn't eat. — special form)
Wiil-ka ma arkin. (The boy didn't see. — special form)

Notice: Past negation often adds -n or uses infinitive!
```

**Past negation: subject + ma + special negative form (-n often added)**

## Slide 16: Double Negatives in Somali

**Somali USES DOUBLE NEGATIVES as standard, unlike English!**

```
ENGLISH (one negative only):
"I don't see nobody." — WRONG!
"I don't see anybody." — CORRECT!

SOMALI (double negatives ARE CORRECT):

TRANSLATION TIP:
When you see double negatives in Somali, understand them as EMPHASIS
not as changing the meaning like in English.
```

**Somali LOVES double negatives for EMPHASIS!**

## Slide 17: Common Double Negative Patterns

**SOMALI DOUBLE NEGATIVES:**

```

INDEFINITES with negation:
Cidina = nobody
Waxna = nothing  
Marno = never
```

## Slide 4: Two Types of Questions

**Somali has TWO main question types:**

1. **YES/NO QUESTIONS:** Answer with "yes" or "no"
   - Aad tag iskuulka? (Do you go to school?)
   - Response: Haa (yes) or Maya (no)

2. **WH-QUESTIONS:** Ask for specific information (who, what, where, etc.)
   - Yuu tag? (Who went?)
   - Response: Wiil-ka. (The boy.)

**Each type has DIFFERENT STRUCTURES!**

## Slide 5: Yes/No Questions

**YES/NO QUESTIONS change WORD ORDER or use QUESTION PARTICLES.**

**Pattern 1: Simple inversion (verb before subject):**

```
STATEMENT (SVO):
Aad tag iskuulka. (You go to school. — statement)

QUESTION (VSO):
Tag iskuulka? (Do you go to school? — question)
Aad tag iskuulka? (Are you going to school?)

MORE FORMAL (with particle):
Miyaad tag iskuulka? (Do you go to school? — with question particle)
```

**Question particle MIY- marks yes/no questions!**

## Slide 6: Question Particles for Yes/No

**MIY- is the PRIMARY yes/no question particle:**

```
STATEMENT:
Isagu wuu tag iskuulka. (He goes to school.)

YES/NO QUESTION:
Miyuu tag iskuulka? (Does he go to school?)
— Note: wuu becomes miyuu

Pattern: [subject] + MIY + [agreement marker] + [verb]

More examples:
Miyad tag? (Are you going?)
Miyay tag? (Is she going?)
Miyay cunay? (Did they eat?)
```

**MIY- replaces or combines with the agreement marker!**

## Slide 9: Wh-Questions — Information Questions

**WH-QUESTIONS ask for SPECIFIC INFORMATION: who, what, where, when, why, how**

**Main interrogatives in Somali:**

| Question | Interrogative | Example | Answer |
|----------|--------------|---------|--------|
| **Who?** | Yuu / Yay | Yuu tag? | Wiil-ka. (The boy.) |
| **What?** | Waa maxay? | Waa maxay? | Waa xariif. (It's a letter.) |
| **Where?** | Halkee? | Halkee buu joog? | Guriga. (Home.) |
| **When?** | Gormee? | Gormee way imaan? | Hadda. (Now.) |
| **Why?** | Maxaaf? | Maxaaf buu tag? | Sababtoo ah... (Because...) |
| **How?** | Sidee? | Sidee buu qor? | Si deg-deg. (Quickly.) |

**Each interrogative has its OWN POSITION in the sentence!**

## Slide 10: The Interrogative YUU/YAY (Who?)

**YUU/YAY is the interrogative for "who":**

```
Yuu = who (masculine subject)
Yay = who (feminine subject)

Examples:
Yuu tag? (Who went? — masculine)
Yay cunay? (Who ate? — feminine)
Yuu qoray? (Who wrote?)

In context:
Yuu qoray xariif-ka? (Who wrote the letter?)
Response: Wiil-ka. (The boy did.)
```

**YUU/YAY replace the subject!**

## Slide 11: The Interrogative WAA MAXAY (What?)

**WAA MAXAY is the interrogative for "what":**

```
Examples:
Waa maxay? (What is it? — general)
Waa maxay xariif-ka? (What is the letter? — what about the letter?)
Waxaan cunaya waa maxay? (What am I eating?)

Responses:
Waa xariif. (It's a letter.)
Waa cuntada. (It's the food.)
Waa qurux. (It's beauty.)
```

**WAA MAXAY asks about IDENTITY or NATURE!**

## Slide 12: The Interrogative HALKEE (Where?)

**HALKEE is the interrogative for "where":**

```
Examples:
Halkee buu joog? (Where does he stay?)
Halkee way socdeen? (Where did they go?)
Halkee baa xariif-ka? (Where is the letter?)

Responses:
Guriga. (At home.)
Iskuulka. (At school.)
Suuqa. (At the market.)
```

**HALKEE asks about LOCATION!**

# MODULE 17: Conjunctions & Discourse

## Slide 1: Title

```
FILIQA SOOMAALI
Somali Conjunctions & Discourse

(Connecting words and linked ideas — sophisticated language!)
```

## Slide 2: Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will:

1. **Use** coordinating conjunctions to link equal ideas
2. **Use** subordinating conjunctions to link dependent ideas
3. **Recognize** discourse markers that signal relationships
4. **Build** coherent discourse (connected speech)
5. **Understand** when to use each type of connector
6. **Create** natural-sounding Somali dialogue

**Key insight:** Conjunctions and discourse markers are the GLUE that holds Somali speech together!

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Conjunctions in Action

**Notice how ideas are CONNECTED:**

```
COORDINATION (equal ideas):
Ani waan tag iskuulka OO adiga waad joog guriga.
(I go to school AND you stay home. — equal ideas)

SUBORDINATION (dependent ideas):
MARKII ani tag iskuulka, way imaan.
(WHEN I go to school, she will come. — dependent)

DISCOURSE MARKER (signaling flow):
Markaa buu tag iskuulka oo way socdeen suuqa.
(THEN he went to school AND they went to the market. — sequence)

CONTRAST:
Ani waan tag iskuulka LAAKIIN adiga ma tagi karaa.
(I go to school BUT you can't go. — contrast)
```

Notice: Conjunctions CREATE CONNECTIONS and FLOW!

## Slide 4: Three Types of Connectors

**SOMALI has three types of words that connect ideas:**

1. **COORDINATING CONJUNCTIONS:** OO, LAAKIIN, HADII KALE, AMI
   - Link EQUAL, INDEPENDENT ideas
   - Both ideas are "main" clauses

2. **SUBORDINATING CONJUNCTIONS:** MARKII, HADII, SABABTOO AH, INTAAD
   - Link DEPENDENT ideas
   - One idea depends on the other

3. **DISCOURSE MARKERS:** MARKAA, WALAACANA, TANA, SIDAAS
   - Signal RELATIONSHIPS between sentences
   - Help with conversation flow and coherence

**Each type serves DIFFERENT PURPOSES!**

## Slide 5: Coordinating Conjunctions

**Coordinating conjunctions link EQUAL ideas (you learned these in Module 14!):**

```
OO = and
LAAKIIN = but
HADII KALE = otherwise
AMI = or

Examples:
Ani waan tag OO adiga waad joog.
(I go AND you stay. — both equally important)

Gabar-ta way cunay LAAKIIN wiil-ka ma cunin.
(The girl ate BUT the boy didn't. — contrast)

Way tagi doonaan iskuulka AMI guriga.
(They will go to school OR home. — choice)
```

**Coordinating conjunctions preserve both clauses' independence!**

## Slide 6: Subordinating Conjunctions

**Subordinating conjunctions create DEPENDENCY (you learned these in Module 14!):**

```
MARKII = when
HADII = if
SABABTOO AH = because
INTAAD = while
INAAN/INAAD = that (purpose)

Examples:
MARKII aad tag, way imaan.
(WHEN you go, she will come. — time dependent)

HADII uu jiro, buu imaan.
(IF he exists, he will come. — conditional)

SABABTOO AH way caafi, way socdeen.
(BECAUSE she was well, they walked. — reason)
```

**Subordinating conjunctions create UNEQUAL relationships!**

## Slide 7: Discourse Markers — Building Coherence

**DISCOURSE MARKERS signal RELATIONSHIPS between sentences and ideas:**

| Marker | Meaning | Example | Use |
|--------|---------|---------|-----|
| **MARKAA** | then, so | Markaa buu tag. | Sequence, consequence |
| **WALAACANA** | so, therefore | Walaacana way imaan. | Logical consequence |
| **TANA** | now, at this point | Tana way socdeen. | Shift in topic/time |
| **SIDAAS** | like that, so | Sidaas way cunay. | Manner, comparison |
| **LAAKIIN** | but (discourse use) | Laakiin way arkisay. | Contrast at discourse level |

**Discourse markers keep conversation FLOWING and COHERENT!**

## Slide 8: Building Coherent Discourse

**Discourse markers CONNECT separate sentences into a cohesive NARRATIVE:**

```
Without discourse markers (choppy):
Ani waan tag iskuulka. Way imaan. Way cunay. Way socdeen suuqa.
(I went to school. She came. She ate. They went to the market. — disconnected)

With discourse markers (fluent):
Ani waan tag iskuulka. Markaa way imaan. Walaacana way cunay.
Tana way socdeen suuqa.
(I went to school. Then she came. So she ate. Now they went to the market. — connected)

Better example:
Markii ani tag iskuulka, way imaan macalimka.
Walaacana way baray caruurta.
Tana way cunay cuntada guriga.
(When I went to school, the teacher came. So she taught the children. 
Now they ate food at home.)
```

**Discourse markers make speech NATURAL and CONNECTED!**

**Key:** Discourse markers create FLOW and CONNECTION!

## Slide 11: Comparing Subordination vs. Discourse Markers

```

Markii ani tag iskuulka, way imaan.

DISCOURSE MARKER (connecting separate sentences):
Ani waan tag iskuulka. Markaa way imaan.
(I went to school. Then she came. — two sentences linked)

Result: Discourse markers feel more NATURAL and CONVERSATIONAL!
```

**Discourse markers are the RHYTHM of natural speech!**

## Slide 4: What Are Adverbs?

**ADVERBS are words that MODIFY VERBS, ADJECTIVES, or OTHER ADVERBS.**

**They answer questions:**

- HOW OFTEN? (frequency) — habaa dhan (always), marjoga (never)
- HOW MUCH? (degree) — aad (very), yar (little)

**Adverbs usually come AFTER the verb (unlike English)!**


**Manner = SI + ADJECTIVE pattern!**

**Key insight:** Somali has DIFFERENT VERBS for being (waa, ahaa, tahay) depending on the CONTEXT!

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Copular Verbs in Action

**Notice the different WAYS TO SAY "IS":**

```
IDENTITY (who/what is it?):
Waa macalim. (He is a teacher.)
Magacaygu waa Fatima. (My name is Fatima.)

STATE/DESCRIPTION:
Gabar-ta way qurux. (The girl is beautiful.)
Wiil-ka wuu cariin. (The boy is strong.)

BECOMING/CHANGE:
Way noqon doontaa macallima. (She will become a teacher.)
Buu noqon jirey xoolo-dheer. (He became tall.)

EXISTENCE:
Jiidka jira macalim. (There exists a teacher.)
Ma jira wax. (Nothing exists = there is nothing.)
```

Notice: DIFFERENT COPULAS for DIFFERENT MEANINGS!

## Slide 4: Types of Copular Verbs

**SOMALI HAS THREE MAIN COPULAR VERBS:**

| Copula | Meaning | Use | Example |
|--------|---------|-----|---------|
| **WAA** | is (identification) | Stating what something IS | Waa macalim. (He is a teacher.) |
| **TAHAY** | am/is/are (state) | Describing state/quality | Way qurux tahay. (She is beautiful.) |
| **NOQON** | become (change) | Expressing change or becoming | Way noqon doontaa. (She will become.) |
| **JIRA** | exist (existence) | Expressing existence | Jira macalim. (A teacher exists.) |

**Each has SPECIFIC uses and DIFFERENT CONJUGATION patterns!**

## Slide 5: The Copula WAA (Identity/Definition)

**WAA is used to IDENTIFY or DEFINE what something IS:**

```
IDENTITY:
Waa macalim. (He/She is a teacher. — what is he/she?)
Magacaygu waa Ahmed. (My name is Ahmed. — what is my name?)
Taas waa duul. (That is a lie. — what is that?)

PROFESSION/ROLE:
Waxaan ahaa xoolo-dheer. (I was a tall one = I was tall. — state)
Waa hawl. (It is work. — what is it?)

STRUCTURE: Subject + WAA + Noun
(Often the subject is UNDERSTOOD/IMPLIED)
```

**WAA = DEFINITION and IDENTIFICATION!**

## Slide 6: The Copula TAHAY (State/Condition)

**TAHAY is used for STATES, CONDITIONS, and QUALITIES:**

```
QUALITY:
Gabar-ta way qurux tahay. (The girl is beautiful. — state of being)
Wiil-ka wuu cariin tahay. (The boy is strong. — state)

CONDITION/STATE:
Way caafi tahay. (She is well. — state of health)
Wuu bogad tahay. (He is tired. — state)

STRUCTURE: Subject + [agreement] + [adj/quality] + TAHAY
```

**TAHAY = CONDITION and QUALITY!**

## Slide 9: The Copula NOQON (Becoming/Change)

**NOQON expresses CHANGE or BECOMING:**

```
PRESENT:
Wuu noqon doona macalim. (He will become a teacher. — change)

PAST:
Way noqon jirtay macallimad. (She became a teacher. — changed)

FUTURE WITH NOQON:
Wuu noqon donaa cariin. (He will become strong. — change over time)

STRUCTURE: Subject + NOQON + [goal/result]
```

**NOQON = CHANGE and BECOMING!**

## Slide 10: The Existential JIRA (Existence)

**JIRA is used to express EXISTENCE or PRESENCE:**

```
EXISTENCE:
Jira macalim dugsi-ga. (There is a teacher at school. — exists)
Jira caruur badan. (There are many children. — exist)

NEGATION (most common):
Ma jira cidina. (Nobody exists = There is nobody. — double negative)
Waxna ma jira. (Nothing doesn't exist = There's nothing. — emphasis)

STRUCTURE: JIRA + [subject] + [location/context]
```

**JIRA = EXISTENCE and PRESENCE!**

## Slide 3: Advanced Agreement Patterns

**Complex sentences require AGREEMENT across multiple elements:**

```
Basic agreement: Gabar-ta (feminine singular) + way (3sg fem) + cunaysay (fem sing)

Complex agreement (gender-number-person chain):
Gabar-ta (fem sing) + way (3sg fem) + cunaysay (fem sing) + cuntada (fem sing def)

When elements don't match (intentional for emphasis):
Gabar-ta oo qurux (agreement on both)
VS
Gabar weyn oo qurux (gabar = fem, but can be used generically)

RULE: All modifiers and verb agreements must match GENDER, NUMBER, and PERSON
```

**Advanced agreement = precision across ALL elements!**

## Slide 4: Focus Strategies Beyond BAA

**You learned BAA for focus (Module 13), but Somali has MORE focus strategies:**

```
FOCUS WITH CONTRAST:
Ani WAA ay tag. (It is I who went. — me, not someone else)

INTENSIVE FOCUS:
Gabar-ta BAA BAA cunaysay. (The girl REALLY was eating. — double BAA for emphasis)

FRONTING WITHOUT BAA:
Cuntada gabar-ta cunaysay. (The food, the girl was eating it. — topicalization)

RELATIVE CLAUSE FOCUS:
Gabar-ta oo cunaysay cuntada baa ah.
(The girl who was eating the food is what it is. — complex focus)
```

**Focus strategies create DIFFERENT LEVELS of EMPHASIS!**

## Slide 5: Topicalization — Making Sentences "About" Something

**TOPICALIZATION moves a topic to the FRONT for emphasis:**

```
NORMAL (neutral):
Gabar-ta way cunay cuntada. (The girl ate food. — neutral)

TOPICALIZED (emphasizing the girl):
Gabar-ta, way cunay cuntada. (The girl, she ate food. — about the girl)

TOPICALIZED (emphasizing the food):
Cuntada, gabar-ta way cunaysay. (The food, the girl was eating it. — about the food)

EXTREME TOPICALIZATION:
Gabar-ta oo cunaysay, taas waa mesha ay joogato.
(The girl who was eating, that's where she stays. — long topic)
```

**Topicalization = telling what the sentence is ABOUT!**

## Slide 6: Register and Style — Formal vs. Informal

**Somali has FORMAL and INFORMAL registers:**

```
FORMAL (literary, official, academic):
Magacaagu maxaa ah? (What is your name? — formal question)
Waxaan sugayaa hadda markaa. (I am waiting, then. — formal/literary)

INFORMAL (conversational, everyday):
Magacaagu waa maxay? (What's your name? — conversational)
Sugaya hadda. (Waiting now. — casual)

DIFFERENCES:
- Word choice (formal: waxaan vs. informal: waan)
- Particle placement
- Tone and intonation
- Sentence complexity
```

**Native speakers CODE-SWITCH between registers!**

## Slide 1: Title

## Slide 2: Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will:

1. **Understand** how Somali creates new words from existing roots
2. **Recognize** derivational suffixes and their functions
3. **Form** agent nouns (one who does X)
4. **Form** patient nouns (thing affected by X)
5. **Derive** adjectives from nouns and verbs
6. **Expand** vocabulary through morphological processes

**Key insight:** Somali is EXTREMELY PRODUCTIVE — you can create new words by adding suffixes to roots!

## Slide 3: Opening Dialogue — Derivations in Action

**Notice how RELATED WORDS come from same ROOTS:**

```
ROOT: qor (write)
Qor = write (verb)
Qore = writer (agent noun)
Qoraal = writing/manuscript (patient noun)
Qoraala = writing pad/place to write (locative)

ROOT: cun (eat)
Cun = eat (verb)
Cune = eater (agent noun)
Cunasho = eating session (abstract noun)
Cuntada = food (what is eaten — patient noun)

ROOT: tag (go)
Tag = go (verb)
Tagi = going (gerund)
Tagida = the going (nominalizing)
```

Notice: ONE ROOT creates MANY RELATED WORDS!

## Slide 4: What Is Derivational Morphology?

**DERIVATIONAL MORPHOLOGY is how Somali CREATES NEW WORDS from existing roots and stems.**

**Three main processes:**

1. **AGENT NOUNS:** Words for "one who does X"
   - Qore = writer (from qor = write)
   - Cune = eater (from cun = eat)

2. **PATIENT NOUNS:** Words for "thing that is X-ed"
   - Qoraal = writing/manuscript (from qor = write)
   - Cuntada = food (from cun = eat)

3. **DERIVED ADJECTIVES:** Words meaning "having quality of X"
   - Cariin = strong (from caar = strength)
   - Qurux = beautiful (from qur = beauty)

**Suffixes are the KEY — adding suffixes changes MEANING and PART OF SPEECH!**

## Slide 5: Agent Nouns — "One Who Does X"

**AGENT NOUN SUFFIXES create words for people who do actions:**

```
-E or -OOL = one who does X

From QOR (write):
Qore = writer

From CUN (eat):
Cune = eater

From TAG (go):
Tago = one who goes

From JOG (stay):
Joge = one who stays

USAGE:
Qore-ta baa xariif-ka qorya. (The writer writes the letter.)
Cunay-da baa cuntada cunaysay. (The eater was eating food.)
```

**Agent suffixes PERSONIFY actions!**

## Slide 6: Exercise 1 — Forming Agent Nouns

## Slide 7: Exercise 1 Answers

2. socod → **socod-ka** (one who walks / walker)
3. bar → **bar-e** (one who teaches / teacher)
4. macquul → **macquul-ka** (one who understands / intellect)

**Key:** Agent nouns add -E, -OOL, or -KA to the verb!

## Slide 8: Patient Nouns — "Thing That Is X-ed"

**PATIENT NOUN SUFFIXES create words for things affected by actions:**

```
-AAL, -ID, -AD = thing that is X-ed

From QOR (write):
Qoraal = writing/manuscript/script (thing written)

From CUN (eat):
Cuntada = food (thing eaten)

From CAR (plant):
Carood = plantation (place planted)

USAGE:
Qoraal-ka baa xariif-ka. (The manuscript is the letter.)
Cuntada-ta way caasaladig. (The food is delicious.)
Carood-da bay gelin. (They entered the plantation.)
```

**Patient suffixes OBJECTIFY actions!**

## Slide 10: Exercise 2 Answers

**Key:** Patient nouns add -AAL, -OD, or -ID to the verb!

## Slide 11: Abstract Nouns — States and Actions

**ABSTRACT NOUN SUFFIXES create nouns for states, actions, and qualities:**

```
-ASHO, -AMO, -NAG = action/state noun

From CUN (eat):
Cunasho = eating (the action of eating)

From BAR (teach):
Baramo = teaching (the action)

From CAAFI (be well):
Caafimad = wellness (the state)

USAGE:
Cunasho-da baa necebtay. (The eating was tasty.)
Baramo-da way wanaagsan. (The teaching is good.)
Caafimad-da iyo nabada. (Health and peace.)
```

**Abstract suffixes NOMINALIZE VERBS into concepts!**

**Key:** Abstract nouns add -ASHO, -AMO, or -NAG to verbs!

## Slide 14: Derived Adjectives

**DERIVED ADJECTIVES are created from nouns using specific patterns:**

```
QUALITY ADJECTIVES:
Cariin = strong (from caar = strength)
Qurux = beautiful (from qur = beauty)
Caafi = well (from caaf = wellness)

LOCATIVE ADJECTIVES:
Baadi-bay = pastoral (from baad = pastoralism)
Magaal-ay = urban (from magaalo = city)

POSSESSION ADJECTIVES:
Halal = permissible (from halag = permission)
Heello = love-sick (from heello = love)

USAGE:
Ninka oo cariin. (The man who is strong.)
Magaalada oo magaal-ay. (The city which is urban.)
```

**Derived adjectives EXPAND DESCRIPTIVE VOCABULARY!**

## Slide 15: Diminutives and Augmentatives

**DIMINUTIVE and AUGMENTATIVE SUFFIXES change the SIZE or INTENSITY:**

```
DIMINUTIVE (-OOG, -AY):
Gabar = girl → Gabar-oog = little girl
Wiil = boy → Wiil-ay = little boy

AUGMENTATIVE (-ED, -WEYN):
Gabar = girl → Gabar-ed = big/grown-up girl
Ninka = man → Ninka weyn = big man

USAGE:
Gabar-oog baa ciyaartay. (The little girl played.)
Ninka weyn baa baro. (The big man teaches.)
```

**Size suffixes add NUANCE and DIMINISHMENT/ENLARGEMENT!**

✅ **ABSTRACT NOUNS:** -ASHO, -AMO (action/state)
- Cunasho = eating, Baramo = teaching

✅ **DERIVED ADJECTIVES:** Qualitative from nouns
- Cariin = strong, Qurux = beautiful

Derivational morphology is how Somali CREATES VOCABULARY!

## Slide 3: Narrative Structure

**NARRATIVES follow a predictable STRUCTURE:**

```
1. OPENING (introduce characters/setting):
Waxaa jiri gabar-gaaban oo magaceedu ay ahaa Fatima.
(There was a young girl named Fatima.)

2. COMPLICATION (introduce problem/challenge):
Markaasay dhowr-jeer door-dor-jay iskuulka.
(Then she struggled to go to school.)

3. DEVELOPMENT (events unfold):
Waxay curin muuqaal ay raadinaysay.
(She was pursuing something she wanted.)

4. RESOLUTION (problem is solved):
Waxaa dhacday inay dhameysatay dugsiga.
(It happened that she finished school.)

5. CLOSING (conclusion):
Markaa waa magaalooyinka wareegtay.
(Then she returned to the cities.)
```

**Narratives move through TIME and CAUSE-EFFECT!**

## Slide 4: Description in Somali

```
SIMPLE:
Gabar-ta way qurux tahay. (The girl is beautiful.)

RICH:
Gabar-ta oo qurux aad u eg, madaxa oo gaaban, 
indhaha oo jiidh, iyo guulka oo muuqata.
(The girl of great beauty, with short hair, large eyes, 
and shining success.)

Guriga baa weyn, hadalkii baa macaan,

(The house is big, the speech is sweet, 
and the door is moonlit water.)
```

**Descriptions CREATE IMAGERY and ATMOSPHERE!**

## Slide 3: Formal vs. Informal Register

**SOMALI has distinct FORMAL and INFORMAL STYLES:**

```
FORMAL (academic, official, written):
Waxaan doon inaan ogaado su'asha. 
(I desire to understand the question. — formal, literary)

INFORMAL (conversational, everyday):
Waxaan raba inaan garto su'aasha.
(I want to get the question. — conversational)

FORMAL:
Mahadsanid aad u mahadsanid.
(I thank you very much. — formal, elaborate)

INFORMAL:
Mahadsanid!
(Thanks! — quick, casual)

FORMAL ADDRESS:
Saaxiibkeyga, waxaan doon...
(My friend, I desire...)

INFORMAL ADDRESS:
Jaahoy, waxaan raba...
(Hey, I want...)
```

**Register is chosen based on CONTEXT!**

## Slide 4: Respectful vs. Casual Language

**RESPECT is shown through LANGUAGE CHOICES:**

```
RESPECTFUL (elder, authority, stranger):
Aad u mahadsanid. (Thank you very much. — formal)
Hadda af somali ma hadalkara? (Do you speak Somali now? — polite question form)
Waxay filayaa. (She thinks. — respecting her view)

CASUAL (friends, peers, children):
Mahadsanid. (Thanks.)
Somali hadalkarta? (You speak Somali? — direct, friendly)
Waxey tiri. (She said. — straightforward)

DISMISSIVE/RUDE (not recommended!):
Ma hadalkarta Somali! (You can't speak Somali! — rude)
Walaac! (Never mind! — dismissive)
```

**Respect is SHOWN through grammar and word CHOICE!**

## Slide 5: Written vs. Spoken Register

**WRITING is more FORMAL than SPEAKING:**

```
SPOKEN (conversational):
Buu tag hadda. Markaa way imaan. Walaacana way cunay.
(He went now. Then she came. So she ate. — natural flow)

WRITTEN (literary, formal):
Markii uu tag iskuulka, way imaan macalimka 
inay barato caruurta.
(When he went to school, she came to teach the children. — complex, formal)

SPOKEN ABBREVIATION:
Waad ogsid? = Waxaad ogsid? (shortened)
Buu yimid = Buu timid (variant)

WRITTEN ELABORATION:
Waxaa jira... (There exists... — formal opening)
Sababtoo ah... (Because... — formal connector)
```

**Writing DEMANDS formality; speaking allows CASUALNESS!**

## Slide 6: Code-Switching in Native Speech

**Native speakers naturally SWITCH between registers:**

```
FORMAL CONTEXT:
Assalaamu alaikum, saaxiibkay.
(Peace be upon you, my friend. — formal greeting)

SAME PERSON, INFORMAL CONTEXT:
Salaam, jaahoy! Iska warran?
(Hi buddy! How are you? — casual)

MIX (code-switching):
Waxaan doon (formal) inaan raba (casual) su'aasha.
(I desire/want the question. — mixing registers)

CULTURALLY APPROPRIATE:
To elders: Formal address, respectful language
To friends: Casual, friendly, sometimes humorous
To strangers: Neutral, respectful, careful
```

**Code-switching is NORMAL and STRATEGIC!**

## Slide 8: Common Mistakes to Avoid

**As you continue learning, watch out for:**

```
MISTAKE: Forgetting gender agreement
INCORRECT: Gabar-ta wuu tag. (girl + he = mismatch!)
CORRECT: Gabar-ta way tag. (girl + she = match!)

MISTAKE: Mixing up tense markers
INCORRECT: Ani waan tag hadii aad tag. (present in conditional = awkward)
CORRECT: Hadii aad tag, buu imaan. (present in condition, future in result)

MISTAKE: Wrong word order for questions
INCORRECT: Yuu tag iskuulka? (who = go school = unclear)
CORRECT: Yuu tag iskuulka? or Halkee buu joog? (clearer)

MISTAKE: Not using BAA for focus
INCORRECT: Xariif-ka gabar-ta qoratay. (unclear what's focused)
CORRECT: Xariif-ka baa gabar-ta qoratay. (focus on letter)
```

**AWARENESS of mistakes helps you AVOID them!**
